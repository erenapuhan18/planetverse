04Cosmic timeline

From a collapsing cloud
to a helicopter on Mars.

Four and a half billion years, compressed into one scroll. The first six entries take longer than everything that follows put together.

  1. FormationSolar nebula

    4.6 billion years ago

    A cloud collapses

    A shockwave — probably from a nearby supernova — tips a cold molecular cloud past the point of balance. It falls inward and begins to spin, flattening into a disc 200 AU across.

  2. FormationT = 0

    4.57 billion years ago

    The Sun ignites

    The centre of the disc reaches 15 million degrees and hydrogen fusion begins. 99.86% of all the mass in the Solar System ends up here. Everything else is the leftovers.

  3. FormationAccretion

    4.55 billion years ago

    Planets accrete

    Dust grains stick, then clump, then collapse into planetesimals. Inside the frost line only rock and metal survive the heat — outside it, ice is available in bulk, and the giants grow fast enough to capture hydrogen.

  4. FormationMoon formation

    4.51 billion years ago

    Theia strikes Earth

    A Mars-sized body hits the young Earth at an angle. The debris ring cools into the Moon within a century. That single impact set Earth's spin, its tilt, and its tides.

  5. Formation

    4.1–3.8 billion years ago

    The Late Heavy Bombardment

    As the giants migrate, they scatter the outer belt inward. The scars are still visible on Mercury, the Moon and Mars, where no weather has erased them.

  6. FormationLife

    3.7 billion years ago

    Something starts replicating

    The oldest widely accepted biosignatures appear in Greenland rock. Life arrives almost as soon as the surface stops being sterilised — which is either encouraging or a warning about sample size of one.

  7. DiscoveryGalilean moons

    1610

    Galileo turns a telescope upward

    Four points of light move night after night around Jupiter. Something orbits something that is not Earth, and the geocentric model does not survive it.

  8. Discovery

    1655

    Huygens solves the rings

    Christiaan Huygens works out that Galileo's puzzling 'ears' are a thin flat ring, detached from the planet — and finds Titan in the same season.

  9. Discovery

    1781

    A new planet, by accident

    William Herschel notices a disc where a star should be. Uranus becomes the first planet unknown to the ancient world, and doubles the size of the known Solar System.

  10. DiscoveryMathematics first

    1846

    Neptune, found on paper

    Urbain Le Verrier calculates where an unseen planet must be from Uranus's wobble. Johann Galle points a telescope at the coordinates and finds it within an hour, 1° from the prediction.

  11. Discovery

    1930

    Clyde Tombaugh finds Pluto

    A 24-year-old farm-raised observer blinks photographic plates for ten months and catches a moving speck. Pluto is reclassified as a dwarf planet in 2006 — after New Horizons had already launched toward it.

  12. Space AgeOrbit

    1957

    Sputnik 1

    An 84 kg aluminium sphere beeps for 21 days and changes what the sky means. Within four years there is a human in orbit.

  13. Space Age

    1969

    Apollo 11

    Armstrong and Aldrin land with 25 seconds of fuel margin. Six landings follow; twelve people walk on another world, and nobody has been back since 1972.

  14. Space AgeGrand Tour

    1977

    Voyager 1 and 2 launch

    A planetary alignment that recurs every 176 years lets two spacecraft visit four giant planets on one tank of fuel. Both are still transmitting.

  15. Space Age

    1990

    The Pale Blue Dot

    At Carl Sagan's request, Voyager 1 turns around at 6 billion km and photographs Earth: a single pixel, caught in a scattered sunbeam.

  16. Space Age51 Peg b

    1995

    The first exoplanet around a Sun-like star

    51 Pegasi b is a Jupiter-mass planet orbiting its star in four days. Every model of planet formation has to be rewritten. The count today is over 6,000.

  17. Modern era

    2012

    Curiosity lands — and Voyager 1 leaves

    A sky crane lowers a one-tonne rover into Gale Crater. In the same year, Voyager 1 crosses the heliopause and becomes the first human object in interstellar space.

  18. Modern era

    2015

    Pluto has a heart

    New Horizons flies past at 14 km/s and finds nitrogen glaciers flowing across Sputnik Planitia. A world written off as a frozen rock turns out to be geologically active.

  19. Modern era

    2017

    Cassini's Grand Finale

    Out of fuel and unwilling to risk contaminating Enceladus, Cassini dives into Saturn, transmitting atmospheric data until the signal cuts out.

  20. Modern eraM87*

    2019

    The first image of a black hole

    The Event Horizon Telescope links radio dishes across the planet into one Earth-sized instrument and resolves the shadow of M87*, 55 million light-years away.

  21. Modern era

    2021–2022

    James Webb opens its mirror

    Launched on Christmas Day 2021, 344 single-point failures unfold perfectly. Six months later it delivers the deepest infrared image of the universe ever taken.

  22. Modern era

    2024

    Europa Clipper departs

    The largest planetary spacecraft NASA has ever built leaves for an ice moon with more liquid water than every ocean on Earth.

  23. What comes next

    2026

    BepiColombo reaches Mercury

    After eight years and nine gravity assists, two orbiters separate and settle into complementary orbits around the least-visited rocky planet.

  24. What comes next

    2028 → 2034

    Dragonfly flies on Titan

    An octocopter the size of a car will hop between organic dunes in a nitrogen atmosphere four times denser than ours, at −179 °C.

  25. What comes nextMars Sample Return

    2030s

    Samples come home

    Perseverance's sealed cores are waiting in Jezero Crater. Returning them means the first round trip to another planet — and laboratory instruments no rover could ever carry.

  26. What comes next

    The long term

    Habitable Worlds Observatory

    The next flagship telescope is designed to do one thing: image Earth-sized planets around Sun-like stars directly, and read their atmospheres for oxygen, water and methane at once.

Black hole

Sagittarius A*

The supermassive black hole at the centre of the Milky Way · 26,000 light-years

Four million Suns compressed inside a region smaller than Mercury's orbit. Nothing escapes past the event horizon, but the gas falling in glows across the spectrum — and the black hole's gravity bends the light of everything behind it into a ring. The Event Horizon Telescope imaged that ring in 2022.

06Mission gallery

Everything we have sent,
and where it is right now.

Thirteen missions that rewrote the textbooks — from two spacecraft still transmitting after forty-eight years to a rotorcraft being built for Titan.

  • Operating1977

    Voyager 1 & 2

    NASA · Outer planets → interstellar space

    The farthest human objects, still answering.

    • Use a once-per-176-year planetary alignment for a Grand Tour
    • Return the first close images of Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune
    • Measure the boundary where the solar wind meets interstellar space
    • Carry the Golden Record — 116 images and the sounds of Earth

    Both craft still transmitting, 48 years on

  • Mission complete1997

    Cassini–Huygens

    NASA · ESA · ASI · Saturn, its rings and moons

    Thirteen years in orbit, and a deliberate death to protect a moon.

    • Orbit Saturn and survey the ring system in detail
    • Land the Huygens probe on Titan — the most distant landing ever made
    • Sample the Enceladus plumes and detect an ocean of liquid water
    • Fly between the planet and the rings to weigh them

    Ended 15 Sep 2017 by design, diving into Saturn

  • Operating2011

    Curiosity

    NASA · JPL · Gale Crater, Mars

    A nuclear-powered geologist that proved Mars was habitable.

    • Determine whether Gale Crater ever supported microbial life
    • Analyse rock chemistry with an onboard laser and laboratory
    • Characterise radiation levels for future human crews
    • Read the climate record written in Mount Sharp's layers

    Climbing Mount Sharp past sol 4,500

  • Operating2020

    Perseverance & Ingenuity

    NASA · JPL · Jezero Crater, Mars

    The first flight on another world, and the first samples set aside for return.

    • Seek signs of ancient microbial life in a river delta
    • Cache sealed rock cores for a future sample-return mission
    • Demonstrate powered flight in a 1% atmosphere (Ingenuity: 72 flights)
    • Make oxygen from Martian CO₂ with the MOXIE experiment

    Exploring the crater rim; samples cached and waiting

  • Operating2021

    James Webb Space Telescope

    NASA · ESA · CSA · Sun–Earth L2, 1.5 million km out

    A 6.5 metre gold mirror, cooled to −233 °C, reading the infrared sky.

    • Observe the first galaxies formed after the Big Bang
    • Take transmission spectra of exoplanet atmospheres
    • See through dust into star-forming regions
    • Study Solar System bodies in the thermal infrared

    Operating with fuel for 20+ years

  • Operating2011

    Juno

    NASA · Jupiter — polar orbit

    Threading a 5,000 km gap between the radiation belts, 60+ times.

    • Map Jupiter's gravity and magnetic fields to find the core
    • Measure water and ammonia deep in the atmosphere
    • Image the poles and their geometric cyclone clusters
    • Fly past Ganymede, Europa and Io at close range

    Extended mission through 2025, flying close moon passes

  • Operating2006

    New Horizons

    NASA · Pluto and the Kuiper Belt

    Nine years of silence, then thirteen thousand images of Pluto.

    • Perform the first reconnaissance of Pluto and Charon
    • Fly past the Kuiper Belt object Arrokoth — the most distant flyby ever
    • Measure the dust and plasma of the outer Solar System
    • Map the hydrogen wall at the edge of the heliosphere

    Beyond 60 AU, studying the outer heliosphere

  • Operating2018

    Parker Solar Probe

    NASA · The Sun's corona

    The fastest object ever built, flying into the corona behind a carbon shield.

    • Fly through the corona to find what heats it to a million degrees
    • Trace the origin and acceleration of the solar wind
    • Survive 1,400 °C behind a 11.4 cm carbon-composite shield
    • Sample magnetic switchbacks in situ

    Record perihelion: 6.1 million km, at 692,000 km/h

  • In cruise2024

    Europa Clipper

    NASA · Europa, moon of Jupiter

    Fifty flybys of an ice moon with twice the water of Earth.

    • Measure the thickness of the ice shell and the ocean beneath
    • Determine the ocean's salinity and depth with radar and magnetometry
    • Sample any plume material vented into space
    • Assess whether Europa has the ingredients for life

    In cruise after a March 2025 Mars gravity assist; arrives 2030

  • In cruise2023

    JUICE

    ESA · Ganymede, Callisto, Europa

    The first spacecraft that will orbit a moon of another planet.

    • Enter orbit around Ganymede, the only moon with a magnetic field
    • Characterise the icy Galilean moons as potential habitats
    • Study the Jovian system as a miniature Solar System
    • Sound the subsurface oceans with ice-penetrating radar

    Venus flyby completed 2025; Jupiter arrival 2031

  • In development2028

    Dragonfly

    NASA · APL · Titan, moon of Saturn

    A nuclear-powered rotorcraft that will fly from dune to dune on Titan.

    • Fly a car-sized octocopter across Titan's organic dunes
    • Sample prebiotic chemistry in a nitrogen atmosphere
    • Search for chemical biosignatures in Selk Crater
    • Study Titan's methane cycle from the surface

    In build; launch 2028, arrival 2034

  • In cruise2018

    BepiColombo

    ESA · JAXA · Mercury

    Two orbiters riding one ion-powered cruise stage to the innermost planet.

    • Deliver two orbiters to complementary Mercury orbits
    • Map the surface composition and the offset magnetic field
    • Test general relativity in the Sun's strong gravity
    • Explain how a planet so small kept an active dynamo

    Final flybys complete; orbit insertion late 2026

Example “Why is Venus hotter than Mercury?”

knowledge base

Because Mercury has no blanket, and Venus has a very thick one.

Mercury is nearly twice as close to the Sun and its daytime surface does reach 430 °C — hotter than anywhere on Venus at that moment. But Mercury has no atmosphere. The instant the Sun sets, all of that heat radiates straight back to space and the surface falls to −180 °C.

Venus is wrapped in 92 bars of carbon dioxide. CO₂ is transparent to incoming sunlight but opaque to the infrared the surface tries to emit, so the heat cannot leave. The result is a runaway greenhouse that holds the whole planet at 464 °C — day side, night side, poles, everywhere. Venus is not hotter because it absorbs more. It is hotter because it never lets go.
